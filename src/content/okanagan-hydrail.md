## Overview

For an engineering economics course at UBCO, my team defended the position that the Okanagan Hydrail tram-train is economically feasible, in a formal debate in front of the class. The concept, known as OVER PR (Okanagan Valley Electric Regional Passenger Rail), is a 342 km hydrogen fuel-cell tram-train corridor from Kamloops to Osoyoos, running on the existing CN freight corridor and the Highway 97 right-of-way.

We did not invent the numbers. Our job was to dig through the published feasibility research, understand the economics well enough to argue them under pressure, and hold the case together against rebuttals on capital costs, operating costs, ridership, and unproven technology.

## The Economic Case

The core figures come from the peer-reviewed OVER PR studies (Boray, Hegazi, Busche & Lovegrove, 2024):

- Net Present Value of roughly $40 billion CAD over a 30-year horizon at a 6% discount rate
- Benefit-Cost Ratio of 9:1, against 2.5 to 3.5 for typical successful North American light rail
- Internal Rate of Return of 33%
- Risk-tested with Monte Carlo simulation and reference-class forecasting, and still viable under a 70% cost-overrun scenario

My contribution focused on interpreting what these indicators actually mean for a decision-maker, and defending them. A BCR of 9:1 counts time savings, avoided highway expansion, emissions reductions, crash reduction, and tourism, not just farebox revenue.

## Cost Analysis

The baseline capital estimate is about $1.5 billion, rising to about $2.9 billion with engineering and risk contingencies built in:

- Track: about $650 million
- Twelve stations: about $240 million
- Rolling stock (Alstom Coradia iLint class fuel-cell units): $50 to $125 million
- Service yard: $70 million
- Okanagan Lake bridge retrofit: $150 million
- Engineering and contingency at roughly 20%

That works out to under $9 million per kilometre. Canadian LRT projects average $104 to $168 million per kilometre, largely because hydrail avoids catenary wires, substations, tunnelling, and land expropriation by using existing corridors. Operating costs are estimated around $164 million per year, dominated by staffing.

## Demand and Delivery

The published studies project ridership growing from roughly 13,000 to 15,000 daily passengers to about 70,000 by 2059, with farebox revenue around $27 million per year by 2050. Resident surveys across the Okanagan consistently show about 65% of respondents want to reduce car use.

Delivery is phased: a roughly 60 km Kelowna to Vernon corridor first, projected to reach a positive return on investment within 10 years, then expansion. Financing follows a P3 model, with tax authority and subsidy mechanisms that are permitted for local governments under BC law.

## Why Hydrogen

Part of my research covered the hydrogen side of the case:

- Fuel cells generate electricity through an electrochemical reaction, not combustion, so the trains are self-powered and need no overhead electrification
- Regenerative braking cuts energy and hydrogen use by about 19%
- Consumption is around 0.17 to 0.25 kg of hydrogen per kilometre, with one refuel per round trip
- A 16-train fleet needs about 5 tonnes of hydrogen per day, which matches BC's current green hydrogen capacity, delivered initially by tube trailer from Prince George

## What I Took From It

This project made me apply engineering economics to something real: reading NPV, IRR, and BCR results critically, framing costs and benefits across direct, indirect, and externality categories, and understanding how sensitivity analysis and reference-class forecasting handle uncertainty in megaprojects. Arguing it live also meant knowing the weak points of the case as well as the strong ones.
